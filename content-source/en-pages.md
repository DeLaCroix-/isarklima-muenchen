# Air conditioning installation in Munich

A successful split air conditioning project starts with the building, not with a product catalogue. Room use, heat gain, possible unit positions, permissions, pipe routes, condensate drainage and the electrical interface all affect what can be installed. IsarKlima plans single-split and multi-split air conditioning for apartments, homes, offices and smaller commercial spaces in Munich, with each project shaped around the actual property.

## Air conditioning for apartments, homes and businesses

The right arrangement depends on how many rooms need cooling and how independently they are used. A compact apartment, a family home and a small office may have very different heat loads, operating patterns and options for an outdoor unit. The installation concept should therefore be based on the actual rooms and building constraints rather than on a standard package.

### Single-split systems for one room

A single-split system connects one indoor unit to one outdoor unit and is intended to cool a defined room or open area. It can be a focused option for a bedroom, living room, home office or another space with a clear cooling requirement. Capacity, airflow, condensate drainage and the route between the two units still need to be assessed for the individual room.

### Multi-split systems for multiple rooms

A multi-split system connects several indoor units to one outdoor unit, allowing separate rooms to be controlled individually. It can reduce the number of outdoor units, but it also requires more detailed planning of capacity, pipe routes, drainage and access. The number and use of the rooms should be established before deciding whether multi-split is preferable to separate single-split systems.

### Solutions for offices and small commercial spaces

For offices and smaller commercial premises, occupancy, equipment, solar gain, opening hours and the way different areas are used all influence the solution. Planning these factors together helps create comfortable conditions without relying on a generic system or capacity assumption.

## How your air conditioning installation works

The installation process moves from an initial project description to a defined technical scope. An on-site assessment is used when photographs and plans cannot confirm the mounting surfaces, route, drainage gradient, outdoor location, access or nearby constraints. ### Installation, testing and handover

Once the scope has been agreed, the work can cover mounting the units, installing the specified lines and connections, completing the required checks and commissioning the system. The handover should explain basic operation and identify the documents relevant to the installed equipment and work. Split air conditioning affects more than the room in which the indoor unit is fitted. Indoor units need a position that distributes air without causing avoidable draughts, while outdoor units need ventilation, service access and a location that limits noise and vibration effects. # Split air conditioning installation in Munich

Split air conditioning can provide targeted comfort cooling without a central duct network, but the apparent simplicity of the system should not obscure the planning involved. The indoor and outdoor units, refrigerant lines, condensate route, power supply and permissions form one installation. IsarKlima plans single-split and multi-split systems around the rooms, building and installation route.

## Single-split or multi-split?

The basic choice is whether one defined area needs an independent system or several rooms need individual indoor units. The answer depends on room use, simultaneous demand, available outdoor space and the routes that can be installed. Neither arrangement is universally better; the building and cooling objectives determine which one is more appropriate.

### Single-split for one room

A single-split arrangement pairs one indoor unit with one outdoor unit. It is suited to a clearly defined room or open zone where independent control is required. Planning still needs to account for air distribution, capacity, outdoor-unit placement, the wall penetration and condensate drainage, so the shortest route is not automatically the best overall position.

### Multi-split for multiple rooms

Multi-split connects several indoor units to a shared outdoor unit and allows rooms to be controlled separately. It can be useful where several rooms need cooling but outdoor-unit locations are limited. The combined capacity, permitted unit combinations, pipe lengths, simultaneous use and maintenance access all need to be checked as part of one system design.

## Which system suits your building?

Product type alone cannot answer whether a split system fits a property. Room loads, unit locations, structural routes and drainage determine how the equipment can be integrated. A good concept balances cooling performance with practical installation, access and the effect on occupants and neighbours.

### Room use and heat gain

A bedroom used at night has a different load pattern from a glazed living room, an occupied office or a room with heat-producing equipment. Floor area is only one input; orientation, glazing, insulation, occupancy and operating hours also matter. These details help avoid choosing capacity from a simple square-metre rule that ignores how the room actually behaves.

### Positioning indoor and outdoor units

The indoor unit should distribute air through the occupied area while remaining accessible for normal user care and future professional access. The outdoor unit needs unobstructed airflow, a stable mounting location and consideration of noise, vibration, appearance and permissions. Both positions must be assessed together because each constrains the connecting route.

### Pipe route and condensate drainage

The route normally carries insulated refrigerant lines, control or power connections as specified, and a condensate drain where required. It should be planned to protect drainage fall, avoid unnecessary length and limit disruption to finished surfaces. If gravity drainage is not possible, any alternative must be assessed explicitly rather than assumed after mounting has begun.

## What does the installation include?

The installation scope should describe the work required to turn specified equipment into a commissioned system. It must distinguish the core refrigeration and mounting work from electrical, access, making-good or other building work. This prevents the word “installation” from hiding exclusions that are essential to completing the project.

### Mounting the units

Mounting includes setting out and securing the indoor and outdoor units on surfaces and supports suitable for the agreed positions. The method depends on the building fabric, vibration control, weather exposure and access. Any facade, roof, balcony or elevated work must be included only after the relevant permissions and safe working method have been established.

### Refrigerant lines and leak testing

The refrigerant circuit must be installed using components, joints and procedures appropriate to the selected system. The connected circuit is then checked using the required technical process before commissioning. The responsible qualified party and the exact test and documentation scope must be confirmed for the project rather than inferred from generic marketing language.

### Commissioning and user handover

Commissioning verifies that the installed system starts and operates under the conditions available at handover and that relevant settings and drainage can be checked. The user handover should cover basic controls, modes and the documents supplied with the installation. It is not a substitute for the manufacturer’s instructions or for any future service requirements stated for the equipment.

## Requirements before the installation date

Installation should not be scheduled until the site is ready for the agreed work. Permissions, unit locations, access, electrical responsibility and exclusions need to be settled in advance. A clear preparation stage reduces the risk of discovering on the day that a route, support or connection cannot be used.

### Permissions and access

Written landlord or owners’ association approval may be needed depending on ownership and the parts of the building affected. Access must cover the rooms, proposed route, outdoor position and any common areas required to bring in equipment or work safely. Keys, site contacts, building rules and working-hour restrictions should be agreed before the appointment.

### Electrical interface

The system’s electrical requirements must be compared with the available supply and the manufacturer’s installation requirements. Where a new circuit, isolator, protection or fixed connection is needed, responsibility for that work must be assigned to an appropriately qualified electrical party. The quote should identify whether this work is included, separately coordinated or excluded.

### Agreed scope of work

The agreed scope should identify the equipment, unit locations, principal route, included installation steps, tests, handover documents and any making-good. It should also list exclusions and client responsibilities, such as permissions or access. This written boundary is particularly important where refrigeration, electrical and building work meet.

## Split air conditioning FAQs

The following answers address the central technical and practical questions that arise before a split installation is specified.

### What is the difference between single-split and multi-split?

Single-split uses one indoor unit and one outdoor unit for a defined room or zone. Multi-split connects several indoor units to a shared outdoor unit so that multiple rooms can be controlled separately. Multi-split may reduce the number of outdoor units, but it requires coordinated capacity and more extensive routing. The best option depends on room use, simultaneous demand, available outdoor positions and the permitted combinations of the selected system.

### How is the right capacity determined?

Capacity should be based on the cooling demand of the specific room, not floor area alone. Relevant inputs include size, orientation, glazing, insulation, ceiling height, occupancy, equipment and operating hours. The proposed indoor-unit position and airflow also matter. A technical assessment combines these factors with the performance characteristics of the system being considered.

### Where can the outdoor unit be installed?

Possible locations can include a suitable balcony area, facade position, terrace, roof or ground-level base, but none is automatically acceptable. The location must provide airflow, stable support, drainage where relevant, safe access and consideration of noise, vibration, weather and external appearance. Ownership, landlord or owners’ association approval and any building-specific restrictions must be clarified before the position is confirmed.

### How is condensate drained?

Condensate from the indoor unit is normally directed to an approved drainage point using a route with reliable fall. The outlet must not create water damage, staining, icing or nuisance to neighbours or public areas. Where gravity drainage cannot be achieved, an alternative such as a suitable pump may need assessment, but its noise, access and failure implications should be considered before it is selected.

### May I connect the system myself?

The equipment may be purchased by a customer, but work on the refrigerant circuit and commissioning is not a DIY connection task. It must be carried out by appropriately qualified and, where applicable, certified persons or businesses under the rules applying to the system and refrigerant. Fixed electrical work also belongs with the relevant qualified trade. Buying equipment first can create compatibility and documentation problems, so the complete scope should be checked beforehand.

# Air conditioning for apartments in Munich

Installing split air conditioning in an apartment requires technical planning and a clear understanding of who can approve changes to the building. The indoor unit may be inside the private dwelling, while the outdoor unit, facade, balcony or wall penetration can affect rented property or common ownership. The process should therefore begin with permission and a realistic installation route, not with ordering equipment.

## Rental or owner-occupied apartment: clarify permission first

The route to approval differs between a rented apartment and a condominium, but in both cases written clarity is preferable before technical work is booked. An outdoor unit or penetration can change the building fabric or appearance even if the indoor equipment is entirely within the apartment. The proposed position, route, noise treatment and condensate solution help the decision-maker understand what is being requested.

### Landlord’s consent

A tenant should present the landlord with a concrete proposal rather than a general request for “air conditioning”. Useful information includes the unit positions, wall penetration, external appearance, pipe route, condensate discharge and how the installation would be fixed. Written consent should be obtained before installation; any conditions concerning restoration, contractors or documentation must be reflected in the project scope.

### Owners’ association resolution

In a condominium, work may affect common property or the building’s external appearance even when it serves one apartment. The proposed facade, balcony or wall work should be submitted through the appropriate owners’ association process, with property management involved where applicable. Whether a formal resolution is needed depends on the measure and property context, so the approval position should be clarified before a date is agreed.

## The right split system for your apartment

Apartment layouts often make room-by-room priorities more useful than an attempt to cool every space indirectly. The choice between single-split and multi-split should consider closed doors, room use, outdoor-unit options and the routes available through finished areas. Cooling one key room well can be more realistic than expecting one indoor unit to control an entire apartment.

### Cooling one room

A single-split system can target the room with the highest or most important cooling demand, such as a bedroom or main living area. The indoor position should support air circulation without directing a continuous draught at beds or seating. The feasibility of the outdoor position and a discreet, drainable route remains just as important as the room itself.

### Controlling several rooms separately

Several indoor units allow bedrooms, living areas or workspaces to be controlled individually, which can suit rooms used at different times. A multi-split system may connect them to one outdoor unit, subject to system limits and route feasibility. Each room still needs its own capacity and airflow assessment; an equal unit in every room is not automatically an appropriate design.

## Positioning the outdoor unit responsibly

The outdoor unit is usually the most sensitive part of an apartment project because it can be visible, audible and located on or near common property. A position should be assessed for airflow, access, support, vibration transfer and its effect on neighbours. Permission and technical suitability must point to the same location before it is accepted.

### Facade, balcony or suitable installation area

A facade position may offer a direct route but can affect appearance, common property and working-at-height requirements. A balcony can provide access, yet still needs sufficient airflow, stable support and compliance with ownership rules. Any other installation area must be evaluated for the same technical and permission constraints rather than chosen only because it is out of sight.

### Noise and vibration

Outdoor-unit sound can be amplified by hard surfaces, corners or lightweight structures, while vibration can travel through brackets and building elements. Position, mounting system and separation from sensitive windows or neighbouring spaces should be considered together. Published equipment data is useful, but it does not by itself predict the sound experienced at a particular property boundary or room.

### Safe condensate drainage

Condensate must be routed to a suitable point without dripping over the facade, onto another property or across a walking surface. The drainage route needs adequate fall, protection and access for the arrangement selected. Where a pump is considered, its location, sound and accessibility should be assessed as part of the installation rather than added as an afterthought.

## Apartment installation process

The process is designed to resolve ownership and building constraints before surfaces are drilled or equipment is mounted. Documents and photographs support an initial review, followed by route agreement and an on-site check where necessary. Installation proceeds only against a defined scope and approved positions.

### Reviewing documents and photos

Useful material includes a simple plan, room dimensions, photographs of the rooms and balcony or facade, and any landlord or owners’ association conditions already received. Images should show the wider context as well as close details, because access and neighbouring windows can matter. This review helps identify missing information; it does not replace an on-site check where measurements or building fabric remain uncertain.

### Agreeing the installation route

The route should show where the wall penetration, insulated lines, drainage and covers will run and how they reach the outdoor unit. It must work technically while limiting disruption to finishes and common areas. Any visible trunking, balcony route or facade element should be included in the material presented for permission so that the approved and installed versions do not diverge.

### Installation and handover

Installation follows the approved positions and written scope, including the assigned refrigeration, electrical and building tasks. Required checks and commissioning are completed before the basic controls and supplied documents are handed over. If site conditions differ materially from the agreed information, the change should be assessed and approved rather than improvised during the work.

## Apartment air conditioning FAQs

Apartment enquiries often turn on approval and outdoor-unit placement as much as on cooling capacity. These answers set out the information to resolve first.

### Do I need my landlord’s permission?

You should obtain written landlord consent when installation affects the building fabric, exterior or any part you are not authorised to alter independently. A useful request explains the indoor and outdoor positions, wall penetration, pipe route, condensate handling, mounting and visible finish. The landlord may impose conditions, and those conditions must be understood before installation is ordered or scheduled.

### Does the owners’ association need to approve?

Approval may be required if the outdoor unit, facade work, wall penetration, balcony arrangement or other elements affect common property or the building’s appearance. The exact process depends on the property and proposed measure. Provide sufficiently specific technical information to the property management or owners’ association and obtain the required decision before treating the installation route as available.

### Can the outdoor unit be placed on the balcony?

A balcony can be possible, but it is not automatically suitable or permitted. The unit needs airflow, stable support, acceptable sound and vibration behaviour, safe drainage and access. Its position must also comply with the landlord or owners’ association decision and must not obstruct escape, use of the balcony or other building requirements. The complete context has to be reviewed.

### How can noise and vibration be reduced?

Reduction starts with selecting a position away from acoustically sensitive rooms and avoiding corners or surfaces that can amplify sound. Suitable supports and vibration isolation can limit structure-borne transmission when correctly chosen for the unit and substrate. Airflow must remain unobstructed, and sound data should be considered alongside the actual distances, reflections and expected operating periods at the building.

### What documents should I prepare before enquiring?

Prepare the full address, a floor plan if available, approximate room sizes, photographs of indoor and outdoor areas and a note explaining which rooms need cooling. Add your tenancy or ownership status and any written landlord, property management or owners’ association information. If a specific installation position has already been discussed, include that context without assuming it is technically approved.

# Home air conditioning installation in Munich

A house offers more options for indoor and outdoor unit placement than many apartments, but it also presents a wider range of room loads and possible routes. A bedroom under the roof, a glazed living area and a ground-floor home office do not need the same solution. The air conditioning should therefore be planned around the rooms that actually require cooling, the construction of the house and the practical path for each connection.

## Cooling one room or several living areas

The first decision is whether cooling is needed in one priority area or across several zones that are used independently. Open doors and staircases do not guarantee useful cooling in remote rooms, so the expected result should be defined room by room. Single-split and multi-split systems provide different ways to meet that need without assuming that every part of the house must be treated alike.

### Single-split for one specific area

A single-split system can serve a bedroom, living room, attic room or home office with its own indoor and outdoor unit. It is most effective when the intended cooling area is clearly defined and air can circulate within that space. The indoor position, outdoor location, pipe route and condensate solution must still be planned as a complete installation.

### Multi-split for multiple zones

Multi-split can connect indoor units in several rooms to one outdoor unit, allowing each zone to be controlled separately. It may be useful where outdoor space or visual impact limits the number of external units. The design needs to account for the capacity assigned to each room, simultaneous operation, pipe lengths and the locations available for routing through the house.

## Matching capacity and unit positions to your needs

Capacity and placement should be developed together. A correctly sized unit in a poor position can create uneven temperatures or intrusive airflow, while a convenient outdoor location can impose an impractical route. The assessment considers how the rooms gain heat, how occupants use them and how the equipment can be integrated without compromising access or neighbouring properties.

### Room size, use and solar gain

Floor area is only the starting point. Glazing, orientation, insulation, ceiling height, roof exposure, occupancy and electrical equipment can materially change the cooling demand. Operating patterns also matter: a bedroom used overnight differs from a sunlit living room used throughout the day. These inputs support a capacity assessment specific to each proposed indoor unit.

### Air distribution in the room

The indoor unit should send conditioned air through the occupied area without blowing continuously at beds, desks or seating. Walls, furniture, beams and door positions can interrupt circulation, and cooling should not be expected to travel reliably around closed corners into other rooms. Placement is therefore chosen for the actual layout, not simply for the shortest external route.

### Outdoor location and neighbours

The outdoor unit needs free airflow, stable support, weather-suitable positioning and access for the installation. It should also be assessed in relation to neighbouring windows, terraces, property boundaries and sound-sensitive spaces. A visually hidden position is not necessarily acoustically or technically suitable, particularly if walls or corners reflect sound or restrict discharge air.

## Preparing installation in an existing or new building

Existing houses require routes that respect finished surfaces and unknown construction details, while new builds offer the chance to coordinate openings, services and finishes earlier. In both cases, unit positions and responsibilities should be confirmed before work reaches a stage where changes become disruptive. Early planning is valuable even though the split equipment itself may be selected later.

### Planning pipe routes early

An early route plan can reserve wall penetrations, sleeves, service zones and suitable paths for insulated lines and drainage. In an existing house, it helps compare visible trunking with more involved concealed work before either is assumed. In a new build, the route should be coordinated with structure, airtightness, insulation and interior finishes rather than added independently.

### Power supply and condensate

The electrical requirements of the proposed system must be matched to the planned or existing supply, with any fixed work assigned to the qualified electrical party. Condensate needs a safe destination and a route with suitable fall or an assessed alternative. Both interfaces should be planned before walls and ceilings are closed or unit positions are treated as final.

## How the system is installed

The installation sequence follows a technical pre-check, a written scope and confirmed access. Mounting, connection, checking and handover are related stages, but other trades or preparatory work may be required between them. The project description should make these boundaries visible before an installation date is set.

### Technical pre-check

The pre-check reviews room loads, unit positions, route dimensions, mounting surfaces, drainage, power and outdoor access. It also identifies permissions or specialist work that must be completed first. Clear photographs and plans can support this stage, but site measurements are needed where the building or route cannot be confirmed remotely.

### Mounting and connection

The units and specified supports are mounted in the agreed positions, and the refrigerant, condensate and control connections are installed along the defined route. Electrical and building interfaces are completed by the responsible qualified parties according to the agreed division of work. Any material change in route or position should be reviewed before it is executed.

### Testing and user handover

The connected system undergoes the checks and commissioning steps applicable to the installed equipment and scope. The handover covers basic controls, relevant operating information and the documents supplied for the project. It should also make clear which future care belongs to the user and which activities require appropriately qualified professional work.

## Home air conditioning FAQs

These questions address how room coverage, building age and outdoor placement influence a house installation.

### Is one indoor unit enough for the whole house?

Usually, one indoor unit should be understood as cooling the room or open zone in which it is installed. Air does not move predictably through closed doors, around stairs or into separate bedrooms, and temperature differences can remain significant. An assessment should identify the priority rooms and realistic airflow rather than promise whole-house cooling from a single point.

### When does multi-split make sense?

Multi-split can make sense when several separate rooms need individual control and a shared outdoor unit is preferable or necessary. It is particularly relevant when outdoor positions are limited, but the permitted unit combinations, total demand, pipe routes and simultaneous operation must work for the selected system. Separate single-split systems may be more appropriate in other layouts, so the decision is project-specific.

### Where should the outdoor unit be placed?

The preferred location combines unobstructed airflow, stable mounting, practical pipe routing, safe access and consideration for neighbours. Ground, wall, terrace and roof positions each create different structural, acoustic and access questions. The final choice should be based on the property, permissions and technical data, not solely on keeping the unit out of view.

### Can air conditioning be installed in an older building?

An older building can be suitable for a retrofit, but construction, protected elements, wall materials, electrical capacity and feasible routes need checking. Finished surfaces and hidden services can make the installation more involved than in a prepared new build. Feasibility should be established from the actual house, and any conservation or ownership restrictions must be resolved separately where relevant.

### What should be planned early in a new build?

Identify the rooms to be cooled, likely indoor and outdoor positions, pipe routes, wall or roof penetrations, condensate drainage, electrical provisions and access. These items should be coordinated with structure, insulation, airtightness, facade design and interior finishes. Early provision preserves options; it does not remove the need to select and assess the final system before installation.

Air conditioning for an office or smaller commercial unit must reflect the way the space is occupied and operated. ## Cooling for offices and small commercial spaces

Some projects involve one enclosed room with a clear heat problem, while others contain several work or customer zones with different schedules. The installation concept should identify those zones and the effect of doors, partitions and internal gains. It should not assume that one unit at the entrance can serve a divided workplace evenly.

### Cooling individual rooms

A single-split system can provide independent cooling for a meeting room, office, ordinary workspace or another defined non-critical area. ## Adapting the system to use and operating hours

Commercial comfort depends on when heat is generated and when people are present. A space may have a moderate morning load and a much higher afternoon load, or one room may be occupied only for short periods. Occupants, computers, displays, lighting and other equipment release heat into the room. Indoor units should be placed with attention to workstations, meeting areas and customer-facing zones, while outdoor units must be considered in relation to neighbours and building users. Sound levels depend on operating mode, distance and the acoustic environment, not only a value in a product table.

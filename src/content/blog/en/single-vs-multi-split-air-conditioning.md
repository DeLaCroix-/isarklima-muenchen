Single-split and multi-split describe two basic layouts for permanently installed air conditioning. They differ mainly in how many indoor units connect to each outdoor unit. The right choice cannot be derived from total floor area alone. It depends on the rooms, simultaneous use and building constraints.

## The technical difference

Both layouts use a closed refrigerant circuit to move heat between the inside and outside. Work on that circuit requires appropriate qualifications. Germany’s current Chemicals Climate Protection Ordinance requires competent persons for the activities it covers; this is not simply a general DIY connection. The official requirements are set out in [section 5 ChemKlimaschutzV](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/chemklimaschutzv_2026/__5.html).

The words single and multi do not by themselves establish suitable capacity, real-life efficiency or whether a proposed location can be approved. Rooms, pipe routes, the outdoor unit and the operating profile must be assessed together.

### One indoor unit with one outdoor unit

A single-split system connects one indoor unit to one outdoor unit. It is a logical option when one clearly defined room or zone is the priority, such as a bedroom, living room or home office. The two units are selected and controlled as one matched pair.

This direct relationship can be useful: if the system is not used or is unavailable, no second indoor unit is affected. Several independent single-split systems, however, require several outdoor units and suitable positions for each. Whether those positions are acceptable on a facade, roof or balcony requires a separate building, ownership and noise assessment.

### Several indoor units connected to one outdoor unit

A multi-split system connects several indoor units to one common outdoor unit. Rooms can be controlled separately while fewer units are visible outside. This may suit homes where several bedrooms or workspaces need regular cooling but the usable external area is limited.

Planning is more complex. Each room needs a suitable indoor unit and its own route back to the outdoor unit. The manufacturer’s permitted combinations, pipe lengths, height differences and outdoor-unit capacity must all correspond. Not every combination that appears possible is technically approved.

## Criteria for the decision

A sound comparison begins with a room schedule. Record the use of every room, expected operating periods, solar gain, glazing, occupants and internal heat sources. It also matters whether several rooms will normally be cooled together or mostly at different times.

The Bavarian consumer advice centre describes split systems as requiring more planning and installation than portable monoblock units, while generally providing stronger performance and less indoor noise. Its current guidance is available under [Installing air conditioning](https://www.verbraucherzentrale.bayern/wissen/energie/energetische-sanierung/klimaanlage-installieren-was-ist-zu-beachten-10525).

### Number and use of rooms

For one enclosed room, single-split is often the natural starting point. An indoor unit in a corridor is not automatically a substitute for units in several bedrooms: doors, airflow paths and different heat gains limit distribution.

Where several rooms are used regularly, multi-split may be appropriate. It can still be useful to compare it with two independent systems, which may better suit the building or operating pattern. There is no universal ranking.

### Outdoor space, pipe routes and controls

Available outdoor space may influence the decision more strongly than the internal floor plan. Several single-split systems require several structurally suitable, accessible and acoustically acceptable positions. Multi-split reduces the number of outdoor units but brings several pipe runs to one location.

Condensate must be discharged safely from every indoor unit. Gravity falls, visible covers, pumps and maintenance access affect the result. In rental and condominium buildings, the approved number of outdoor units and approved pipe route are fundamental constraints.

## Considering installation from the outset

System selection and installation design belong together. Before choosing equipment, possible indoor positions, the outdoor location, pipe routes, condensate drainage, the electrical interface and required permissions should be known. Only then is it possible to assess whether single-split, multi-split or a combination fits technically and organisationally.

Product selection should also consider current and forthcoming refrigerant rules. EU Regulation 2024/573 introduces phased restrictions for certain split systems. The specific system type, capacity, refrigerant and date of placing on the market are relevant. See [Regulation (EU) 2024/573](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/573/oj?locale=en).

## FAQs

### Is multi-split always more efficient?

No. Efficiency depends on the specific products, sizing, part-load behaviour and operating pattern. The number of outdoor units alone does not answer the question.

### Can one indoor unit cool several rooms?

Air can move through open doors, but that does not automatically provide reliable and separately controlled cooling for several enclosed rooms.

### Is multi-split always cheaper?

No. Equipment, the number of indoor units, pipe lengths, access and installation work determine cost. A project-specific comparison is required.

### Can another indoor unit be added later?

Only if the outdoor unit, available connections, capacity and manufacturer approval allow it. Future expansion should not be assumed without a technical check.
